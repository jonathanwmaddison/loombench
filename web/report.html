Peer influence between LLM agents with assigned personas and objectives

A study in the LoomBench environment of how far one agent's stated preference changes another agent's decision, and what that implies for multi-agent collaboration.

11 October 2026 · model jev-1.13.0 · protocol adapted from Laustsen et al., “Peer Influence across Heterogeneous AI Models” (arXiv:2610.03095) · interactive data: Peer influence · system: Design

Abstract. Five agents driven by one model act in a shared environment in which they compete for, concede and share a fixed resource over 26 rounds. Each agent is assigned a one-sentence persona and, for four of the five, a one-sentence objective. Before each action the receiving agent is queried, then queried again; in a randomised 75% of decisions the second query carries one sentence stating the action that an adjacent agent would have it take. Influence is the change in the probability the receiver assigns to that advocated action.

Across two runs of the same 22 match designs (5,436 decisions, 3,976 with sender and receiver in disagreement), a single unreasoned message raised the probability of the advocated action by 27.9 [25.6, 30.3] percentage points relative to the repeated query, and made it the receiver's modal choice in 46% of cases (control: 2%). The two runs gave 27.9 and 27.9 points. Influence varied with the receiver's prompt. Under one persona (blunt) there was no net shift toward the advocated action (−0.3 [−5.2, 4.6]); under the other ten the shift ranged from 22 to 43 points. Receivers with an objective concerning the contested resource shifted 20 points less than receivers with none. Senders did not reliably advocate actions that served their own objective. With peer communication agents conceded more in both runs; no difference in the rate at which objectives were attained was detected.

In this setting a peer's bare recommendation was a strong intervention on a decision, its effect depended on the receiver's prompt, including a sentence written for another purpose, and less conflict between agents did not coincide with better attainment. The study uses one model and one environment; Section 6 lists what it cannot show.

1. Question

Multi-agent systems route the output of one model into the context of another. Laustsen et al. show that when two LLMs disagree on a classification, exposure to the other's answer shifts the receiver's answer substantially, and that the size of the shift depends on the pairing of models. Their agents are different models with no stake in the answer. This study asks a related question of agents that are the same model under different prompts, and that have something at stake:

  1. How much does one message from an adjacent agent change a receiver's action probabilities?
  2. How does that vary with the receiver's persona and objective, with the sender's, and with what is advocated?
  3. Do match-level outcomes differ when agents communicate?

The first question is answered by a randomised comparison within matches. The second is answered by subgroup descriptions and the third by comparing whole executions; both are weaker evidence, and are labelled as such below.

2. Method

2.1 Environment

Five agents are seated side by side at a loom. Each holds a contiguous share of its width (100 units in total; 20 each at the start) and weaves that share in its own design. A match has 26 rounds. In each round every agent chooses one action: maintain position, claim share from an adjacent agent, concede share to one, or propose joint ownership of a strip. An agent whose share is claimed may accept, refuse or counter-propose joint ownership; a refused claimant may override the refusal, withdraw or compromise. Code computes the legal actions, writes them as sentences and resolves every interaction; the model returns a probability for each option, and one option is sampled (temperature 0.7). The full call sequence is documented in Design.

2.2 Agents

A persona is one sentence in the wording of standard personality inventories, describing one pole of one of the five major trait dimensions (outgoing/reserved, warm/blunt, organised/careless, anxious/calm, curious/conventional), or is absent. It says nothing about the task. The labels name the sentence, not a measured trait: no check was made that a sentence produces the disposition it describes, and one wording per persona was used. An objective is one sentence: Grow (finish with the largest share), Give (finish with the smallest share), Hold (finish with exactly the initial share), Borrow (weave designs adopted from other agents), or none. In every match the five agents hold the five objective levels, one each; personas are assigned by a balanced schedule in which each persona holds each objective once per 11 matches. A match design is one such assignment of personas and objectives to seats, with its random seed.

2.3 Conditions

No communication: the environment as described. Peer communication: before each action, the procedure of Section 2.4. The same 22 match designs were run under both conditions.

2.4 Protocol for one decision under peer communication

1 · Prior
The receiver is queried for its action. The returned probabilities over the available actions are the prior.
2 · Sender
One adjacent agent, drawn at random, is queried under its own persona and objective: which of the same actions would it have the receiver take? Its modal choice is the advocated action.
3 · Posterior
The receiver is queried for its action again. Treatment (probability 0.75): the query carries one sentence stating the advocated action. Control (0.25): it carries no message. The match proceeds from this response.

The two arms of step 3 differ only in the message. The message is composed by code from the text of the advocated option; the model returns probabilities, not text, so the message carries no justification and no attribute of the sender other than its name. Options are presented in the same order in every query. The query of step 1 also asks the round's design questions in the same request; the query of step 3 asks for the action only, in both arms.

2.6 Data

Two runs, each with the 22 match designs executed without communication, without communication a second time (a replicate) and with peer communication. The runs are not independent samples: they use the same match designs and differ in one rule of the environment. Run 2 uses the current rules (a fixed palette and a distinct starting design for each agent); Run 1 used the earlier rules, under which agents chose their first design. Their agreement is a replication across a small change of environment. Each run of the peer condition contributes about 2,700 decisions: 5,436 in total, of which 3,976 (73%) were discordant (2,925 treatment, 1,051 control). No call in the runs analysed fell back to a default. The no-communication replicate of Run 2 covers 16 of the 22 match designs (Section 6).

2.7 Analysis

Decisions are nested within agents and matches, so intervals are computed over clusters, not decisions: the mean of cluster means with a Student-t 95% interval. Because the two runs share their match designs, a cluster in a pooled estimate is a match design (k = 22), or an agent of a match design for estimates by persona (k = 10) or by objective (k = 22), containing the decisions of both runs. Estimates are given as mean [95% interval], with the two runs shown separately beside them.

The primary result is the treatment effect, which is randomised within matches. Estimates by persona, objective, advocated action and prior certainty are subgroup descriptions: the assignment is balanced, but ten agents per persona do not separate a persona from its seat and from the other agents present, and no correction is made for multiple comparisons. Every number in this report is printed by analysis/peers.mjs or analysis/outcomes.mjs.

3.6 Receivers with a less certain prior shift further

When the receiver's prior assigned less than 0.6 to its modal action, the treatment effect on influence was 34.0 [30.5, 37.5] pp and adoption under treatment 67%; at 0.6 or more, 22.4 [19.6, 25.3] pp and 26%. The difference between the two effects is 11.6 [7.1, 16.1] pp. A receiver with a confident prior is thus moved less, but still by more than 20 points. Part of the difference in adoption is arithmetic: in a discordant decision the advocated action starts below the prior modal action by construction, and starts closer to it when the prior is flat.

These comparisons are weaker evidence than the treatment effect of Section 3.1. Each run has one execution with peer communication, and executions differ with no treatment at all: of the 34 placebo intervals computed (17 outcomes in two runs), 5 exclude zero where about two would be expected, against 12 of the 34 comparisons with peer communication. In Run 2 the placebo difference in the Give score (+15.6 [1.3, 29.9]) is nearly as large as the difference with communication, in the opposite direction.     4. Implications for multi-agent collaboration

    Each implication is stated with the evidence it rests on and a test that would check it in another system. All hold for one model, one environment and one wording of each prompt (Section 6); they are reasons to measure, not general laws.

    A peer's stated preference is an intervention, not context

    Evidence. One sentence with no justification shifted the receiver by 28 pp more than a repeated query and made the advocated action its modal choice in 46% of disagreements (control 2%); where the two already agreed it added 15 pp.

    Implication. In this setting, placing one agent's recommendation in another's context changed the second agent's decision substantially. Where independent judgments are wanted (review, verification, voting), this argues for eliciting them before exposure; a judgment obtained after exposure should not be assumed independent. Test. Record each agent's answer before and after it sees a peer's, with a repeated-query control.

    Susceptibility can be set by the receiver's prompt, unintentionally

    Evidence. A single sentence about general disposition, saying nothing of the task or of other agents, was associated with influence from 0 pp (blunt) to 43 pp (reserved).

    Implication. A sentence written to set tone can change how far an agent conforms to its peers. One wording per persona was tested, so the result shows that such effects can be large, not which instructions produce them: susceptibility should be measured for the prompts actually deployed, per role. Test. Vary only the disposition sentence of the receiving agent and measure the shift.

    An objective that bears on the decision reduces susceptibility, partly

    Evidence. Within match designs, receivers with an objective on the contested resource shifted 20 pp less than the receiver with no objective; receivers with an objective on another dimension shifted at least as much as those with none (48 against 38 pp).

    Implication. A criterion that bears on the decision at hand was associated with less conformity; an objective about something else was not. The reduction was partial: receivers instructed to maximise or to keep their share still adopted advice to concede it in 18% and 19% of cases. Test. Compare shifts for agents with no criterion, a relevant criterion and an irrelevant one.

    Do not assume an agent's requests serve its own objective

    Evidence. Agents instructed to minimise their share asked for share in 68% of messages and for the objective-serving action in 13%. In both runs their final share was higher and their objective score lower with communication (Section 3.7; descriptive).

    Implication. Designs in which agents negotiate, on the assumption that each will press its own interest, depend on the agent translating its objective into the right request of others. That translation failed here for one objective. Test. Classify each inter-agent request as serving, opposing or neutral to the sender's objective.

    An agent that does not shift can still shift others

    Evidence. Blunt agents showed no net shift toward advocated actions (0 pp), while messages from blunt senders shifted receivers by 35 pp (mean across sender personas 31 pp).

    Implication. Susceptibility and influence on others are separate properties, so influence between two differently prompted components need not be symmetric. Whether the asymmetry changes outcomes was not tested: blunt agents held the largest mean final share of any persona in all six executions (34–40%), with and without communication. Test. Measure influence in both directions for every pair of roles.

    Less conflict is not evidence of better performance

    Evidence. With communication, concessions rose in both runs and claims fell; no difference in attainment was detected, and one objective was served worse in both runs.

    Implication. Process indicators (agreement, fewer objections, smoother transcripts) should not be assumed to track outcome. This study does not establish what communication caused at the level of outcomes; it shows that the two can come apart. Test. Evaluate every communication mechanism on task outcome against a no-communication baseline, with a replicate to size run-to-run variation.

    Peer input has most effect where the receiver's prior is flat

    Evidence. The treatment effect was 34 pp when the prior modal probability was below 0.6 and 22 pp otherwise (difference 11.6 [7.1, 16.1]).

    Implication. A recommendation moved undecided receivers more than decided ones, and decided receivers still moved. The environment has no correct action, so nothing here bears on whether peer input corrects or introduces errors. Test. Condition the shift on prior confidence and, where ground truth exists, on correctness.

    5. Relation to Laustsen et al.

    Laustsen et al. test seven open-weight models on three classification tasks and report that susceptibility varies more than persuasiveness across model-dataset combinations (standard deviations 0.19 and 0.10). The pattern here is similar in kind: the persona means differ more for receivers than for senders (11.4 against 5.6 pp), although the difference rests largely on one persona (Section 3.4).

    The comparison goes no further than that. Heterogeneity here is induced by prompts within one model, not by different models. Their agents exchange a label with an explanation, and they report an ablation without explanations; here the message is a bare recommendation and the model cannot produce an explanation. Their tasks have correct answers and disinterested agents; here there is no correct action and the sender has a stake. Their influence score and the probability shift used here are different measures, so magnitudes are not compared. For reference, the mean shift here is 0.32 of the probability mass available to the advocated action.

    6. Limitations

    • One model. Jev is a classifier that returns probabilities over options written by code. Results may not transfer to text-generating models or to other model families; the central comparison of the source paper, between different models, was not made.
    • One environment. A contested, fixed resource with partly opposed objectives. Influence may differ where interests are aligned or where a correct answer exists.
    • One sentence, one exchange. No justification, no reply, no multi-turn discussion. The message reveals nothing of the sender but a name.
    • Personas are prompt sentences. Each is a single sentence describing one pole of one trait, in one wording. The labels are not validated measures of a disposition, and combinations were not tested.
    • 22 match designs. The two runs share them, so pooled intervals rest on 22 clusters (10 agents per persona). Persona-level intervals are wide, subgroup estimates are descriptive, and multiple comparisons are uncorrected.
    • The two runs differ in one rule (how an agent's first design is set). Their agreement is a replication across a small change in the environment, not an exact replication and not an independent sample.
    • Match-level inference. Each run has one execution with peer communication, and differences between two executions of a match design arise without any treatment (Section 3.7). Outcome-level statements are descriptive.
    • The first and second queries differ in form. The first asks the round's design questions in the same request; the second asks for the action only. The control arm, which has the same form as the treatment arm, shows no shift.
    • Incomplete data. The Run 2 no-communication replicate has 16 of 22 match designs, and planned reference runs with uniform objectives were not executed, because the API credit was exhausted. Matches affected by failed calls were discarded.

    7. Reproduction

    node analysis/peers.mjs       # every decision-level estimate, from the records in runs/ and runs-v1/ (no key needed)
node analysis/outcomes.mjs    # the match-level comparisons of Section 3.7
python3 analysis/report.py    # this page, from the two files those write

node --env-file=<file with TYPESAFE_API_KEY> tools/run.mjs --matches 1-22 --run main          # no communication
node --env-file=<file> tools/run.mjs --matches 1-22 --run replicate                        # its replicate
node --env-file=<file> tools/run.mjs --peers --matches 1-22 --run peers                    # peer communication (about $0.02 a match)

    Run 2 is in runs/main, runs/replicate and runs/peers; Run 1 in runs-v1/. Every decision of the peer condition, with prior, sender and posterior probabilities, is in runs/peers/peers.json; every response of every match is in its record's tape, from which the match re-executes without the model.
